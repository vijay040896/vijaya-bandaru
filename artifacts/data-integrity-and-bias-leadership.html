Data Integrity as a Foundation for Ethical AI

Objective

I created this artifact to connect two pieces of work that turned out to be more related than they first appeared: a hands-on data-cleaning project for a concession-sales dataset, and a leadership reflection on navigating human bias in AI. My objective was to show that the discipline of rigorous data cleaning isn't just a technical exercise, it's the same discipline that prevents biased data from becoming biased AI in the first place.

Process

On the technical side, I audited a 214-row raw sales dataset and found five categories of problems: duplicate and colliding transaction IDs, missing values, inconsistent formatting (misspelled items, inconsistent capitalization), outliers and impossible values (a quantity of 500 sold, a $99.99 price on a $3 item), and calculated fields (Revenue, Actual_Profit) that didn't match their own formulas. I fixed each category using evidence already present in the data, cross-referencing fields against each other, rather than guessing, and documented every fix in a change log. I then applied PivotTable-style summaries, a live SUMPRODUCT array formula, and a Solver-style optimization model to the cleaned data.

On the leadership side, I worked through a class discussion on how human bias enters AI systems, specifically through data selection, labeling, algorithm design, testing and evaluation, and interpretation of results. Two of the field-specific strategies I developed from that discussion are the direct source of the connections below:

Strategy — Scrutinize the data behind every AI Skill, both its source and its labels: Appian's Document Extraction & Classification AI Skill depends entirely on labeled training data. Whoever labels those documents brings their own judgment calls, so labeling guidelines need periodic review, and ambiguous cases should be labeled by more than one person to catch individual bias before it becomes the model's baseline.

Strategy — Treat algorithm and vendor selection as a bias decision, not just a technical one: choosing between an Appian-native AI Skill and an external vendor model isn't neutral; different models are trained on different data and carry different embedded assumptions, so vendor selection should include a basic bias comparison, not just accuracy and cost.

Work Sample — Connection 1: Labeling Bias and Row-Level Cross-Checking

The labeling strategy above is precisely the failure mode I was hunting for, row by row, in the concessions dataset: values that looked plausible on their own but didn't hold up once cross-checked against other fields (a category that didn't match its item, a margin that didn't match its profit). The technical habit of not trusting a value until it's been checked against independent evidence is the same habit that catches a mislabeled training example before it becomes a model's baseline assumption.

Work Sample — Connection 2: Interpretation Bias and the Outliers That Look Fine

Several of the worst errors in the raw dataset didn't look extreme in isolation, a $2.50 unit price, an 80% margin stored as text, a category that was merely "inconsistent" rather than obviously wrong. Each one only became visible once I checked it against a second, independent fact (the item's approved price, the row's own Actual_Profit). That's the same discipline my "interpretation of results" strategy is built on: resisting the pull to accept an AI output because it looks fine at a glance, and instead logging and checking the cases that are inconvenient or ambiguous rather than waving them through.

Tools

Python (pandas) and Excel for the data-cleaning and PivotTable/SUMPRODUCT/Solver analysis; Claude (Anthropic) to help draft and refine the bias-leadership value statement and strategies.

Value Proposition

This artifact shows a range I don't think either piece would show alone: the technical rigor to actually find and fix real data-quality problems, and the leadership awareness to explain why that rigor matters ethically, not just technically. Concretely, if I were auditing an Appian AI Skill's document classification output and it looked fine at a glance, the same discipline I used to catch a mismatched category or a corrupted margin in the concessions data is exactly what I'd apply there: check the classification confidence against the document's source or format, not just its stated result, because a quiet skew toward one source type is the kind of error that hides in plain sight the same way a $2.50 price hid in a column of otherwise-correct numbers. That's the kind of range I want my portfolio to demonstrate: not just knowing that bias matters, but having the row-level habits that actually catch it.
